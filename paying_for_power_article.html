Why does industrial electricity cost so much in Britain?

On the survey averages of the largest consumption bands, industrial electricity in Britain costs roughly three times what it does in China. But there is no single British price — the number changes with firm size, relief eligibility and how power was bought. This essay takes the bill apart — wholesale power, networks, levies, margins — to find which parts are actually responsible, and tests the popular explanations against the evidence.

1The argument we are testing

A large online debate holds that high energy prices in Europe — Britain and Germany especially — are making manufacturing uncompetitive against China and driving deindustrialisation. The most common diagnosis blames net-zero policy: levies added to bills to fund the renewables rollout, plus rising network costs as grids are rebuilt around intermittent generation and utilisation falls. The most common prescriptions follow directly — restart domestic oil and gas production, or build nuclear, to bring prices down.

This essay tests those claims. Rather than relying on survey averages, we rebuilt the industrial electricity bill bottom-up for the UK, Germany and China from published tariff schedules, levy rates and market prices, for two model factories, mirrored across all three countries at equivalent connection levels:

2What industry actually pays — and what it makes

Start with observed prices: what statistical offices record industry as paying. For the UK that is DESNZ's Quarterly Energy Prices survey (largest consumption bands, including the Climate Change Levy at the rate firms actually bore it); for Germany, Eurostat's industrial bands (nrg_pc_205); for China — which runs no equivalent survey — the documented tariffs themselves: provincial catalogue price schedules until October 2021, and thereafter the grid companies' monthly agency-purchase price notices and NDRC's provincial transmission & distribution price schedules, weighted by provincial consumption — with one provenance warning: the Chinese line is a documented-tariff construct, not a survey, and from 2022 its medium-band series rests on Jiangsu alone (the large series keeps a three-province basket). Chinese prices have fallen steadily — and kept falling after the October 2021 market reform (NDRC 1439号) — while UK prices climbed through the 2010s and then jumped in the gas crisis. By 2025 a very large UK consumer paid about $282/MWh against $163 in Germany and $87 in China; the ranking is identical for mid-sized sites, at higher levels throughout.

Below sits the output of the energy-intensive industries themselves — a value-weighted composite of steel, aluminium, cement and paper tonnage. UK output has roughly halved since 2010; Germany has lost about a fifth; China's has grown by about two-thirds. The correlation is stark, but it is not yet a diagnosis: electricity is one candidate among several — demand, trade exposure, carbon policy, exchange rates, Chinese overcapacity — and this essay attributes bills, not closures. To know what the bill itself can explain, we must open it.

The pairing is the argument. British manufacturing as a whole grew by roughly a quarter over these fifteen years. The four commodities halved. Whatever went wrong was not general industrial decline — it was specific to the electricity-intensive, internationally traded end of production. Germany shows a different shape again: manufacturing flat and commodities down a fifth, a broader and shallower problem. China rose on both.

One qualification. On a wider definition of energy-intensive industry — the five divisions that include chemicals and refining, the aggregate Destatis publishes and the one an economist would reach for first — UK output is not down at all. It is up about 20% since 2010, and Germany's is down 17% (both read at 2024, the last year the narrow composite also reports). Both readings are true of Britain, because the divisions bundle the electricity-intensive primary stages that left with the downstream fabrication that stayed: the published index for “basic iron and steel” also contains tube-making, cold rolling and wire drawing. The claim this piece defends is therefore the narrow one. Not that energy-intensive industry has halved, but that the most electricity-intensive, most traded part of it has, while the rest held.

3Anatomy of a bill

To explain the divergence we decompose each bill into three modelled components, on identical definitions across countries:

Wholesale. The energy itself, bought at market prices. In Britain and Germany the marginal price is usually set by gas plant; in China largely by coal contracts.

Network, system & balancing. Transmission and distribution charges (TNUoS/DUoS; Netzentgelte; 输配电价) plus system operation and balancing costs.

Levies & taxes, incl. generation-capital recovery. Policy costs recovered through bills: the schemes that pay for the renewables rollout (RO, CfD, FiT; EEG, KWKG, offshore levy; China's government funds and surcharges) plus electricity taxes.

Supplier retail costs & margin. Not part of any published tariff schedule. For the UK we measure them directly from Ofgem's Consolidated Segmental Statements and show them as a fourth segment (2012–24). For Germany we show the same segment on the same definition from BNetzA’s “Vertrieb und Marge” — $20.0/MWh for a site taking 24 GWh a year, against the UK’s $17.5/MWh in 2024; 2025 is its first published year, so it is held constant across the chart. China publishes nothing equivalent, so there the whole block stays inside the gap to the observed line — section 6 analyses that gap.

The chart below shows the stack before any exemption, with the observed price drawn over it. Two things stand out. First, the UK levy wedge: policy costs grew from about $20/MWh in 2010 to a peak near $77 in 2020 — within it, generation-capital recovery alone rose from $9 to $64 — so that by 2025 an unexempted UK site faced $71/MWh of levies against $36 in Germany and $11 in China. Second, wholesale: crisis years aside, UK and German wholesale costs are similar (~$100–106 in 2025), while China's sits near $60. On pre-exemption terms, the UK was actually cheaper than Germany for most of the 2010s; the ranking flipped around 2021.

One boundary holds throughout: this is an accounting of what appears on the bill, not of the full economic cost of each country’s electricity policy. When Germany moved the EEG levy to the federal budget in mid-2022, the cost moved to taxpayers rather than out of existence; when Britain exempts its energy-intensive industries, the exempted revenue is recovered from every other consumer; and China’s regulated tariffs embed policy choices — coal price bands, cross-subsidy between user classes — that never appear as line items. A cheaper bill is not always cheaper policy.

4The relief layer: what heavy industry is excused from

Neither Britain nor Germany actually charges its heaviest industry the full stack. The UK exempts eligible energy-intensive industries (EIIs) from the renewables levies — 85% from 2018, 100% since April 2024 — excludes them from the Capacity Market charge, and under the British Industry Supercharger compensates network charges: 60% from 2025, rising to 90% from April 2026. Separately — the piece most commentary misses — energy used in mineralogical and metallurgical processes has been exempt from the Climate Change Levy since April 2014: cement, lime, glass and ceramics on one side, steel, aluminium and metal processing on the other. A firm outside those processes can hold a Climate Change Agreement instead, which discounts the levy by 92% on electricity rather than removing it — a paper mill, unlike the glassworks or foundry beside it in the medium archetype, takes that route, so the zero-levy exempt stacks below are a floor for it, understating its bill by under a dollar per megawatt-hour. Germany ran the EEG-Umlage cap (BesAR) until the levy moved to the federal budget in 2022, and still grants baseload users the §19 StromNEV network-charge rebate, floored at 10% of the published tariff, alongside electricity-tax reductions. China has no comparable relief layer: the documented tariff is what large users pay.

And British relief, generous on paper, is held by few: suppliers’ Renewables Obligation returns put supply to EII certificate holders at 12.3 TWh in 2024–25 — 15% of the country’s industrial electricity consumption, spread across some 550 firms — of which 12.0 TWh was actually exempted. The other 85% of industrial volume pays the unrelieved levies, which matters whenever this piece reads a survey average against a modelled stack.

The chart repeats the decomposition for a fully exempt site. The transformation is dramatic: UK levies collapse from $71/MWh to nothing at all — the residual used to be the Climate Change Levy, and the heaviest processes are exempt from that too — and relieved network charges converge — by 2025 a large UK EII paid about $5.5/MWh in pure network charges against $7.9 in Germany. For exempt industry, the "levies and grid costs" story has largely been legislated away.

5All-in: what relieved industry actually pays

Strip out everything relief removes and the league table changes. The chart shows the all-in modelled price for a fully exempt site — the card below sets out what “fully exempt” requires in each country: Britain tests how much electricity hurts a firm, Germany tests what the firm is and how it runs, which is exactly why the ranking inverts at medium size. And all-in now means what it says: each national line includes the measured supplier retail costs and margin of sections 3 and 4, the segment China’s regulated tariff already embeds. Through the mid-2010s relieved German industry paid $33–51/MWh less than its British counterpart — the BesAR-era advantage — but for the largest sites that gap closed steadily and has now crossed over: by 2025 a fully relieved British site pays $133/MWh against $141 in Germany ($118 against $121 on the statutory stack alone). For its most favoured firms Britain is no longer merely level with Germany; it is modestly cheaper, and the crossing owes as much to Britain’s own exemptions (section 4) as to anything that changed in Germany. The medium archetype ranks the same way but far more widely ($138 against $187), because a two-shift 20 GWh site fails Germany's §19 StromNEV baseload test and so keeps almost all of its network charges — German relief is generous but narrowly targeted.

China needs more care, because the modelled and the observed pictures appear to contradict each other. On the survey averages of section 2, British industry pays $195/MWh more than the Chinese tariff at large size and $213 more at medium; measured against our unrelieved stack, supplier block included, those gaps fall to $135 and $122; for a fully relieved EII they fall again, to $46 and $29 — mostly the difference between gas-set and coal-set wholesale (about $47 at large size) plus the British supplier block, less network and levy components that relief has actually pushed below China’s. All three are true, of different firms. The narrowest sets a British best case — fully exempt, transmission-connected, flat load — against what any Chinese 大工业 user actually pays, because China has neither a relief tier nor a survey wedge to strip out. So the convergence is real but conditional: it is available only to firms that clear the eligibility tests, and relief moves costs rather than removing them — every exempted dollar is recovered from consumers who do not qualify. Neither caveat changes the finding: for qualifying heavy industry, the UK's remaining price problem is not levies.

6Why bills lag the market: the hedging effect

If the stack is right, why do observed bills diverge from it so violently in crisis years? Most of the answer is procurement timing. The panels below take the fully-exempt stack in both countries and price its wholesale component two ways: at the delivery year's day-ahead spot price, and at the year-ahead forward price at which industrial demand is actually bought. In both countries the spot-priced stack peaks in 2022, when day-ahead prices exploded; but observed bills — and the as-procured stack — peak in 2023, a full year later. The observed line hugs neither vintage exactly, because real portfolios blend one-, two- and three-year-ahead purchases; but its timing follows the forward-priced stack, and it sits between the two in both crisis years. That one-year phase shift is the hedging effect: firms were partly shielded on the way up in 2021–22, and paid above spot on the way down in 2023. Any model priced at a single wholesale vintage will show large, sign-flipping errors in exactly those years — which is what our wedge does.

Did the two countries hedge equally? Not quite. In 2022 — netting off each observed series' normal calm-year distance from its stack — UK prices absorbed roughly 23% of the gap between the as-procured and spot-priced stacks; German ones about 15%. Treat the pair as direction, not measurement — it rests on one crisis year, on that calm-year netting, and on best-case sites read against heterogeneous band averages — and the German figure, its supplier block pinned at 2025, would if anything show less passthrough still. Faster British passthrough is consistent with the shorter hedge horizons of its non-domestic market. But both populations were clearly hedged in both directions — in 2023 the UK still sat $83 above even a spot-priced, margin-inclusive stack, Germany $33 above its spot stack — and the post-2023 divergence in observed levels owes more to policy than procurement: Germany moved the EEG levy to the federal budget in mid-2022 and capped 2023 bills with the Strompreisbremse, both of which sit inside its observed line, while the UK's non-wholesale stack kept growing.

One more feature needs squaring: the German observed line floats a roughly constant $15–65/MWh above the exempt one (supplier block drawn inside, as in these charts; $35–85 against the statutory stack). That is not a model error — it is the relief spectrum. Our solid line is a floor; Eurostat’s band averages firms spread between that floor and no relief at all; the shaded corridor, a partially-relieved firm, runs at or just below the observed line in most calm years. The drivers are structural — which is why the premium is constant: it peaked with the EEG-Umlage in 2014 and compressed once the levy moved to the federal budget.

Timing explains the swings; it does not explain why UK observed prices sit above the modelled line in calm years. Part of that level gap is the suppliers’ own selling costs and margin — but the stacks already draw that block inside the line (sections 3–5), so it cancels between observed and modelled, leaving a premium of roughly $0–40/MWh through the 2010s rather than the $10–50 measured against the statutory stack alone (2018 is the exception either way, dipping just below the line). And what the supplier block is not, mostly, is profiteering: Ofgem's Consolidated Segmental Statements put measured retail costs plus EBIT margin at only £13–19/MWh — in 2024, of a $24 gap to the statutory stack, roughly $8 was operating cost, $9 margin and $6 residual — and margin itself went negative in 2020–21. What is left above the supplier-inclusive line is band composition: the QEP average includes many firms less exempt, and buying less well, than our archetype.

The 2024–25 gap is different in kind: strip the non-wholesale components and the supply block out of the observed bill and the electricity price British industry implicitly paid has sat at roughly £130/MWh for four years straight — through a market that fell 60% — while Germany’s equivalent unwound. That is the signature of contracts struck near the 2022 peak still working through laddered books: procurement vintage, worth very roughly £30/MWh (large) and £60/MWh (medium) of the 2025 gap — brackets, not points: the figure moves a few pounds with how the unmeasured 2025 supply block is imputed. It is a labelled residual rather than a measurement, and it carries a testable prediction — the 2026–27 QEP prints must fall toward the ~£81 front-year price struck in 2025, or the label is wrong. The first print arrived with the June 2026 release, and it moved the right way: first quarter against first quarter, the implied price fell for the first time since the plateau began — £143 to £122 for a large site, £168 to £139 for a mid-sized one — closing roughly a third of its excess over the £81 front-year price in a single year. One provisional quarter is not confirmation; but the survey’s own method says this is where an unwind must show up, because each band’s price is total value over total volume across every contract in force — an average of the whole book, not of new deals.

7Where the wholesale price comes from: gas at the margin

Wholesale electricity is priced hour by hour at the running cost of the last plant needed to meet demand — for a gas plant, fuel at the hub price plus carbon, nothing else, because the plant already exists. That makes the claim “the power price is the gas price” directly testable: reconstruct a gas plant’s marginal cost from published gas and carbon prices and lay it under the day-ahead power price. For Britain the two series are the same line. Excluding the two crisis years, day-ahead power runs a mean of £3.7/MWh above gas marginal cost at 50% efficiency; in 2023–25 the gaps are +£2.4, −£1.8 and −£0.8. At annual resolution there is essentially nothing in the British wholesale price except gas and carbon. The visible excursions are 2016 (French nuclear outages) and 2021–22, when scarcity pricing and within-year shape pushed the market above any annual-average cost line.

Germany runs the same test to a different verdict: gas marginal cost is the ceiling there, not the price. German day-ahead sat €8–18 below gas SRMC through 2012–18 — hard coal and lignite were the marginal units — converged during the 2019–21 fuel-switch era, and has fallen below again since 2023 as renewables claim more of the price-setting hours. Since the two countries buy gas at hub prices that track each other, the UK–German wholesale difference is a merit-order difference, not a fuel-price one. And the carbon term is not a footnote: it is about a quarter of British gas-SRMC in 2023–25 (£24 of £85 in 2025), and the £18 Carbon Price Support on top of the EU price is why coal left the British merit order early — handing gas the price-setting role in virtually every hour, years before Germany’s coal fleet lost it.

Where does levelized cost belong in this story? As the anchor economics, not the price mechanism. China does not run this auction at all: its tariff is anchored on coal that generates at roughly $67/MWh, inside a regulated band, with no carbon cost — against an all-in cost of British gas-fired generation near $186. That anchor difference, not market design detail, is the China gap in section 3. Equally, the cheap renewables in the chart below do not pull the hourly price down while gas remains the marginal unit: their low LCOE governs what gets built and what CfDs cost — a saving that arrives on the levy side of the bill — while marginal cost governs what you pay per hour.

8So which component is to blame?

The answer depends on which comparison you care about. Against China, the gap is mostly the price of energy itself: gas-set wholesale of $100–106/MWh against coal-anchored $59, plus a levy stack China simply does not carry. No relief scheme touches that. Against Germany, for firms outside the relief schemes, the culprit is the levy stack — above all generation-capital recovery, the cost of paying for the renewables buildout, at $71 versus $36/MWh in 2025. For fully exempt heavy industry, however, the popular story is out of date: levies are down to nothing — the exemptions of section 4 in combination — pure network charges have converged with Germany's ($5.5 vs $7.9, with system and balancing costs on top in both countries), and for the large baseload archetype Britain has crossed over: all-in, supplier blocks included, it is about $8/MWh cheaper than Germany. At medium size the ranking inverts far more widely — Britain is nearly $50 cheaper, because German relief largely passes two-shift sites by. The observed premium UK firms pay over the modelled line is not a policy charge at all: it is procurement timing — the hedging legacy of section 6 — and the composition of the survey itself, which averages firms that mostly hold no exemption and buy less well than our archetype. Suppliers’ measured costs and margin are already drawn inside the modelled line, not part of that premium.

But the exempt case is also a minority experience. The certificate covers 15% of Britain’s industrial electricity (section 4), and the British eligibility test is the harder of the two (section 5’s card). Published data cannot separate how much of the remaining 85% is ineligibility from how much is eligible firms never applying — no register of certificate holders exists — but either way, most British industrial electricity is bought at unrelieved rates, which is why the survey averages sit where they do.

So the claims we set out to test come back mixed: renewables levies did drive the pre-relief UK price up, but for the EIIs at the centre of the deindustrialisation debate the binding constraint today is the wholesale price — which section 7 showed is, almost to the pound, the price of gas plus carbon. That reframes the remedy debate. Two proposals dominate it — build nuclear, and drill the North Sea again — and the next two sections test each against the mechanism rather than the slogan.

9What nuclear actually costs — and where

If the long-run answer to a gas-set power price is something cheaper at the margin, nuclear is the only firm low-carbon candidate — so its cost is worth a section of its own. The comparison below puts three kinds of number on one axis. The cheapest tier is not building anything: extending the life of an existing reactor costs $29–41/MWh on the OECD/NEA benchmark, and restarting Germany’s closed fleet is estimated at $42–80 — options Britain does not have, since its retired stations closed at the end of their lives. The middle tier is what new construction costs where building is routine: on the OECD/NEA’s harmonised benchmark — same 7% cost of capital, each country’s own build costs — new nuclear runs from $54 in Russia and $68 in South Korea to $85 in China and India and $91 in France and the United States (the IEA’s newer estimate for China, in section 7’s chart, is $72 — see the note below the figure). The top tier is what the West has actually delivered: Hinkley Point C’s contracted £129/MWh ($170), Vogtle’s ~$176 out-turn, Flamanville’s ~$201 cost requirement, and the British government’s own 2016 first-of-a-kind estimate, $213 in today’s money.

The spread is not technology. France’s EPR models at $91 at benchmark costs, and China built two EPRs at Taishan in nine years; the same design took 17.7 years at Olkiluoto and slipped twelve at Flamanville. The spread is delivery — serial construction by an experienced supply chain against first-of-a-kind projects a generation after the last one — compounded by financing, since at Britain’s ~9% hurdle rates every year of delay capitalises into the strike price. That is why the chart’s top tier sits $80–150 above its middle tier for the same machines. The schedule record below makes the same point in construction time: what each builder promised at first concrete, and what happened.

The chart also corrects a tempting shortcut — that China simply knows how to build and the West simply does not. China’s own 2009-vintage units sit in the slow half of the chart: Taishan and Sanmen were the first EPRs and AP1000s finished anywhere, imported first-of-a-kind designs, and they slipped four to five years — spending nearly as long in construction as Vogtle. What changed by the 2015–2020 starts was not the nationality of the builders but the maturity of the product: the Hualong One is a domestic design built in series, and its newest units — San’ao 1 the latest, this April — deliver in five to six and a half years, the best of them on the schedule stated at first concrete. Even the engineering state did not one-shot nuclear; it bought its learning curve with a decade of late first-of-a-kinds and kept building until the design settled. The experience is partly transferable — the Emirates hired Korea’s production line mid-run and got Barakah built in under nine years with no nuclear history of its own, though Korea’s own Shin Hanul 1 slipped nearly six years in the same period — but there is currently no Western vendor with a warm production line to hire. For Britain the implication is precise: Hinkley is a first-of-a-kind after a generation-long gap, already four to five years behind its own schedule, and what matters most about Sizewell C is whether it becomes the second unit of a series or another first one.

And how would a buildout actually cut the wholesale price? Only by one route: taking hours away from gas. The wholesale price is set by the marginal plant, so new reactors lower it not when they generate but when there are enough of them that gas stops setting the price — the role Germany’s coal fleet played in the 2010s (section 7’s German panel) and France’s reactors played for decades. The cost discipline is unforgiving, because new nuclear is bought on contracts for difference: whenever the market price sits below the strike price, consumers pay the difference as a CfD top-up, so a buildout at too high a strike simply moves money from the wholesale line to the levy line.

For the argument of this essay, the placement against the two reference lines is what matters. At the costs Britain has revealed, nuclear power costs about what British gas-fired power costs — it insures against gas, but cannot undercut it. At the costs the benchmark tier achieves, nuclear sits at or below the Chinese coal anchor — the line the whole China gap of this study hangs from. The difference between those two worlds is not physics; it is a construction industry. Financing is the one lever policy holds directly, and Sizewell C’s regulated-asset-base model — consumers pay during construction in exchange for a lower cost of capital — is the current attempt to pull it.

10What’s left under the North Sea

The other proposed remedy reaches for the gas itself. The data gives it less room than the rhetoric suggests. The UK Continental Shelf still matters to Britain: it supplied 344 TWh of gas in 2024, half of national consumption (50.3%). But both lines slope the same way — production has halved since 2010, consumption is down a third — and the inventory behind them is thinning faster: proven (1P) reserves fell from 3.9 to 1.9 billion barrels of oil equivalent between 2015 and 2024 — from roughly 6,600 to 3,200 TWh of energy content — and the gas share is down to about 1,000 TWh: three years of current production, eighteen months of consumption. At 2024’s production rate of 401 million boe a year, the proven inventory covers under five years — a rolling stock that sanctioning continuously tops up, but the chart below sets the effort against the inventory it is meant to sustain: capital spending has fallen by nearly two-thirds across the decade the reserves series covers, £17 billion in 2015 to £6 billion now in real terms — and by more than that from the £22 billion peak of 2013–14, just before that window opens — with the proven stock following it down.

The often-quoted doubling of unit operating cost — £11 per barrel in 2020 to £19.5 in 2024 (NSTA benchmarking, mixed oil-and-gas basis) — is mostly the same decline seen from the other side: the industry’s total operating spend has been roughly flat in cash terms, £7.2 billion in 2010 and £7.8 billion in 2024, a quarter lower in real terms — so the rise is the denominator shrinking, not the platforms getting dearer to run. Investment could pull the unit number back down mechanically — the 2015–19 ramp of the last big sanctioned fields produced the £11–12 floor that way — but because the therm sells at the market price regardless, a lower unit cost widens the rent, not the bill.

Set against the market that prices Britain’s gas, though, the shelf is small — and that, not the size of the inventory, is what settles the electricity question. Because gas flows to the best price through interconnectors and LNG terminals, an extra British therm displaces an imported one without discounting the marginal therm — so the drilling debate is really about tax receipts, the current account and security of supply, all real, none of them the power price. Even an implausible doubling of UKCS output would move the traded market by low single digits, and the one modern case of a country drilling its way to cheap gas — America’s shale decade — required both production at world scale and export bottlenecks that trapped the gas at home; Britain has neither. The exception that maps the limit: in 2022 Britain’s hub briefly traded below the continent’s — not because of drilling, but because LNG cargoes kept landing while the export pipes to Europe ran full; by 2023 the two hubs were back within a dollar.

The proven inventory is not the whole story — what could more spending unlock? Rather a lot, on paper: the discovered-but-unsanctioned stock is more than three times the proven one. What has stalled is not the geology but the drilling. Exploration’s yield is documented and stable — a ~50% success rate for two decades, historic finding costs of £2–4 per barrel — so the collapse in discoveries (239 mmboe found in 2018, at most 100 a year since 2021, against 401 produced in 2024) is an input story: exploration and appraisal wells fell from 49 in 2010 to 7 in 2024, three of them exploration, a ten-year low. In 2024 exactly two development consents moved fewer than 50 mmboe from contingent to proven; the reserves-replacement ratio was minus 14%.

So yes — more E&P spending would find and sanction more gas; the scenario fans put the range at 2.6 billion boe produced to 2050 with no further sanctioning against 4.3 billion on an accelerated-but-achievable path, with first production typically five years behind a discovery. But every barrel of that range changes the import share, the current account and the duration of the rent arithmetic above — sanctioning the contingent gas would stretch the sell-at-cost runway from eighteen months toward five-plus years — and none of it changes the price of the marginal therm, which the traded market sets regardless of whose gas it is.

What about the cost of extraction itself? A common intuition holds that the British side of the North Sea is expensive to work, and that this is part of the bill problem. Like-for-like unit costs by country are not constructable — the NSTA figure mixes oil and gas, and other jurisdictions publish on different bases — but the comparison that can be made honestly is the delivered cost of a therm to Britain by route, and it points the other way: on the Oxford Institute’s cost stacks, UK domestic gas is among the cheapest routes at under $5/MMBtu, alongside Qatari LNG and Norwegian pipe, while US LNG — the cargo that actually balances Europe’s market — carries a full-cycle cost near $9, of which some $4–5 is liquefaction, shipping and regasification that a domestic therm never incurs. The market price in 2025 was around $12. Every route’s cost sits far below it: the price is set by the dearest supply plus scarcity, and every cheaper route — domestic gas most of all — pockets the difference as rent. Extraction cost is not Britain’s gas problem; who collects the rent is the only live question.

That reframes the most radical proposal — nationalise production and sell domestic gas at cost — as a rent transfer, not a cost reduction, and its ceiling is computable. A therm of UKCS gas sold at 2025’s market price of £30/MWh cost roughly £20 to find, develop and produce (on the mixed oil-and-gas basis that is all the data allows); of the £10 wedge, the 78% marginal tax regime assigns about £8 to the Exchequer and £2 to the producer. The wedge is a 2025 snapshot, not a constant — with the market it has swung the Exchequer’s UKCS take between a £0.4 billion refund and £9 billion a year within the past decade — so the ceiling moves with the price that sets it. Selling the entire 344 TWh of domestic output at cost would therefore transfer at most ~£3.5 billion a year to consumers — about £5/MWh across everything Britain burns — and since the state already collects most of the wedge (£4.5 billion in 2024–25 across all UKCS production, £2.7 billion forecast for 2025–26), the scheme is largely a transfer from taxpayers to gas users, not new value. A state owner would also inherit the basin’s decommissioning liability — a multi-decade obligation the Exchequer already part-funds through tax relief — which the rent ledger above does not count. Norway is the control case: the cheapest producer in Europe sells at market and banks the rent in a sovereign fund rather than discounting the therm. And the duration is capped by the inventory arithmetic above — eighteen months of consumption in proven gas reserves, sustained only by the E&P investment whose returns the scheme would confiscate.

11The levers that remain

Which leaves the levers this study’s own findings point to. In the short run the biggest movable piece is not policy at all but time: the 2022-vintage contracts behind the £130 plateau of section 6 roll off — the first 2026 survey already shows the implied price falling 15–17% year on year — and the 2026–27 full-year prints will show whether bills follow the market down as fast as they failed to follow it up. In the medium term, carbon-price design is a quarter of the marginal cost equation, and relief coverage decides which firms actually receive the convergence documented in section 5. And in the long run the wholesale price stays the gas price until something cheaper sets the margin — which is a construction-cost problem, not a market-design one.
