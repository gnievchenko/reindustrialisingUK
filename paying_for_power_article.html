Why are British industrial electricity rates so high?

Britain’s heavy industry has halved in fifteen years, and Europe is losing energy-intensive production to China. The issue is real and complex; but many online commenters like to reduce the discussion to high industrial electricity prices, and blame net-zero policies for this outcome.

Not satisfied with this overly reductive framing, I set out to uncover how much more expensive European electricity prices really are, what is driving the wedge, and whether popular solutions — a nuclear renaissance, a North Sea drilling campaign, or filling the wires back up — are credible policy answers.

1Industrial archetypes

Industrial activity is a broad term. It encompasses anything from mom-and-pop bakeries on the local high street to multi-acre integrated steelworks. Most countries aggregate industrial electricity statistics into consumption bands — any business drawing between 2 and 20 GWh a year falls into the same one. In addition to generation cost, electricity tariffs typically include network charges, levies, and taxes, which can again differ depending on the user’s voltage, power draw or activity.

Therefore, in attempting to reconstruct a ‘typical’ electricity tariff, one has to define what a ‘typical’ industrial user is. But the truth is there is no ‘typical’ industrial user. Even within the same industry different business models and technologies will change the profile of power demand.

Still, for the purpose of this analysis, I have come up with two archetypes: large, and medium-sized industrial users, which I believe are a close-enough proxy. They differ not only by annual energy consumption, which defines the type of network they would plug into — transmission vs. distribution — but also by their uptime. 2What industry actually pays — and what it makes

To begin, we will look at the records of historical prices paid by industry. I call them ‘observed’ prices. The data can be found in DESNZ’s Quarterly Energy Prices survey (QEP) for Britain and Eurostat’s industrial bands for Germany. China runs no equivalent survey, so its line is built from documented tariffs — provincial catalogue schedules to October 2021, then grid companies’ monthly agency-purchase notices and the provincial transmission & distribution (T&D) price schedules of China’s National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC), weighted by provincial consumption. It is a tariff construct rather than a survey, and from 2022 its medium band rests on Jiangsu alone.

Plotting observed prices, we note that indeed, industrial electricity prices in the UK have risen meaningfully since 2020, both for medium and large-scale industry. Those in Germany have stayed roughly flat. However, we can see that Chinese industrials have benefited from declining prices, and that trend has been running for as long as the time series exists.

Thus we have established that there is indeed an observable divergence in industrial electricity rates. The question then becomes whether it moved in tandem with ‘deindustrialisation’.

Plotting total manufacturing output reveals that despite rising electricity prices, British manufacturing has grown by a fifth since 2010. Counterintuitively, in Germany, where electricity rates stayed roughly flat, output rose until 2018 but has since fallen back, leaving the manufacturing index where it started. In comparison, Chinese industrial output has grown by 174%.

In energy-intensive industries — steel, paper, cement, aluminium — output more closely matches the trends seen in electricity prices. British output has halved since 2010; German output has declined by 19%, while Chinese output has grown by 67%. There is a caveat in that on a wider definition which includes chemicals and refining, UK output would not be down at all; instead, it would be up 20% between 2010 and 2024, against Germany’s 17% decline. I purposefully excluded chemicals given that most of their energy use comes in the form of feedstock.

While broader manufacturing activity has not declined since 2010 in Germany or Britain, it has failed to keep the pace of growth set by China. What’s worse, energy-intensive industries have meaningfully declined. On this basis, it would be fair to infer that higher industrial prices have cost European businesses competitiveness. The question is whether something can be done about it. In the next section, I will examine the anatomy of the industrial bill.

3Anatomy of a bill

To explain the divergence we decompose each bill into components defined identically across the three countries:

Wholesale. The energy itself, bought at market prices — set by gas plant in Britain and Germany, largely by coal contracts in China.

Network, system & balancing. Transmission and distribution use-of-system charges (TNUoS and DUoS in Britain; Netzentgelte; 输配电价) plus system operation and balancing (BSUoS in Britain).

Levies & taxes, incl. generation-capital recovery. Policy costs recovered through bills — Britain’s Renewables Obligation, Contracts for Difference and feed-in tariffs (RO, CfD, FiT); Germany’s EEG, combined-heat-and-power and offshore levies; China’s government funds and surcharges — plus electricity taxes.

Supplier retail costs & margin. In no published tariff schedule, so we measure it — Ofgem’s Consolidated Segmental Statements for Britain, BNetzA’s “Vertrieb und Marge” for Germany: $20.0/MWh there against $17.5 here in 2024, which settles it as a non-differentiator. China publishes nothing equivalent, so the block stays inside the gap to the observed line.

Two features of the pre-exemption stack drive everything that follows. The first is the British levy wedge: policy costs went from about $20/MWh in 2010 to $71 in 2025, against $36 in Germany, almost all of it generation-capital recovery — paying for plant already built. That growth is mechanical, not discretionary: the Renewables Obligation closed to new capacity in 2017, but its generators only come off the scheme between 2027 and 2037, on a buy-out price uprated with inflation each year — an entitlement already granted, not an annual decision. Contracts for Difference top generators up to a fixed strike, so the levy line rises precisely when the wholesale line falls. Germany’s stack shrank for a reason with no British analogue — in mid-2022 it moved the EEG levy off bills and onto the federal budget.

The second is that British and German wholesale costs are close — about $100–106/MWh in 2025 — while China’s sits near $60. That is a difference in what sets the marginal price, and section 6 takes it apart.

One boundary holds throughout: this is an accounting of what appears on the bill, not of the full economic cost of policy. Germany’s EEG transfer moved cost to taxpayers; Britain’s exemptions are recovered from every other consumer; China’s regulated tariffs embed cross-subsidies that never appear as line items. A cheaper bill is not always cheaper policy.

4What relief removes — and where it leaves Britain

Neither Britain nor Germany charges its heaviest industry that full stack. Britain exempts eligible energy-intensive industries (EIIs) from the renewables levies — 85% from 2018, 100% since April 2024 — excludes them from the Capacity Market charge, and compensates network charges at 60%, rising to 90% from April 2026. Separately, and missed by most commentary, energy used in mineralogical and metallurgical processes has been outside the Climate Change Levy since 2014 — cement, lime, glass, ceramics, steel and aluminium. Firms outside those processes hold a Climate Change Agreement instead, which discounts the levy by 92% rather than removing it, so the zero-levy stacks below are a floor for a paper mill. Germany capped the EEG levy (BesAR) until it moved to the budget, and still floors baseload users’ network charges at 10% of the published tariff under §19 of its grid-fee ordinance (StromNEV). China has no relief layer at all.

Britain tests how much electricity hurts a firm; Germany tests what the firm is and how it runs. The British certificate is blind to load shape; the German network floor is not — which is why a two-shift 20 GWh site clears the British test, fails the German one, and why the ranking inverts at medium size.

For a fully qualifying site the levies-and-grid story has largely been legislated away already. UK levies fall from $71/MWh to nothing at all, and pure network charges converge with Germany’s — $5.5/MWh against $7.9 in 2025.

All-in, that inverts the league table. The mid-2010s German advantage has not merely narrowed but crossed: by 2025 a fully relieved British site pays $133/MWh against $141 in Germany ($118 against $121 statutory), and at medium size $138 against $187, because the German site fails the §19 test and keeps almost all of its network charges. The crossing owes as much to Britain’s own exemptions as to anything that changed in Germany.

Two things bound what follows. Relief is a transfer rather than a saving — every exempted dollar is recovered from consumers who do not qualify — and it reaches few firms: suppliers’ Renewables Obligation returns put exempted supply at 12.0 TWh in 2024–25, about 15% of British industrial electricity, across some 550 firms. The other 85% of industrial volume pays unrelieved rates — which is where the survey averages come from, and why the live policy question about relief is now coverage rather than depth.

Against China the same conditioning runs in reverse. On survey averages British industry pays $195/MWh more than the Chinese tariff at large size; against our unrelieved stack, $135; for a fully relieved EII, $46 — mostly the difference between gas-set and coal-set wholesale (about $47 at large size), plus the British supplier block, less network and levy components that relief has pushed below China’s. All three are true, of different firms. What survives every version: for qualifying heavy industry, Britain’s remaining price problem is not levies.

5Why bills lag the market: the hedging effect

If the stack is right, why do observed bills diverge from it so violently in crisis years? Because industrial power is bought forward. The bill reflects the price when contracts were struck, not when the electricity was delivered, so spot-priced stacks peak in 2022 while observed bills peak a full year later. Firms were partly shielded on the way up and paid above spot on the way down. Any model priced at a single wholesale vintage will show large, sign-flipping errors in exactly those years.

The two countries hedged differently but not dramatically so: British prices absorbed 41% of the 2022 gap between the as-procured and spot-priced stacks at the large archetype against Germany’s 18%, consistent with the shorter hedge horizons of the British non-domestic market; at medium size the two are close, 24% against 21%. What separates them after 2023 is policy, not procurement — Germany moved the EEG levy to the federal budget and capped 2023 bills with the Strompreisbremse, both of which sit inside its observed line, while Britain’s non-wholesale stack kept growing. The German observed line floats a constant $15–65/MWh above the exempt one because our line is a floor and Eurostat averages firms spread between it and no relief at all.

Britain’s equivalent premium runs $0–40/MWh, and it is not profiteering: Ofgem’s Consolidated Segmental Statements put measured retail costs plus operating margin at £4–21/MWh across 2012–24 — £6–7 for most of the pre-crisis decade, and margin itself went negative in 2020–21. The stacks draw that block inside the modelled line, so it cancels; what is left is band composition.

The 2024–25 gap is different in kind, and it is the one genuinely short-run lever this study finds. Strip the non-wholesale components and the supply block out of the observed bill and the electricity price British industry implicitly paid has sat at roughly £130/MWh for four years — through a market that fell 60% — while Germany’s unwound. That is the signature of contracts struck near the 2022 peak still working through laddered books: procurement vintage, worth very roughly £30/MWh at large size and £60 at medium. It is a labelled residual, not a measurement, and it carries a falsifier: the 2026–27 prints must fall toward the ~£81 front-year price struck in 2025. The first moved the right way — implied prices down 15–17% year on year in the June 2026 release — and the survey’s own method says this is where an unwind must appear: each band’s price is total value over total volume across every contract in force, an average of the whole book rather than of new deals.

6Where the wholesale price comes from: gas at the margin

Wholesale electricity is priced hour by hour at the running cost of the last plant needed to meet demand — for a gas plant, fuel at the hub price plus carbon and nothing else, because the plant already exists. That makes “the power price is the gas price” directly testable: reconstruct a gas plant’s short-run marginal cost (SRMC) from published gas and carbon prices and lay it under the day-ahead power price. In Britain the two are the same line: excluding the crisis years, day-ahead runs a mean of £3.7/MWh above gas marginal cost at 50% efficiency. At annual resolution there is essentially nothing in the British wholesale price except gas and carbon.

Germany runs the same test to a different verdict: there gas marginal cost is the ceiling, not the price, because hard coal and lignite set the margin through 2012–18 and renewables have claimed more of the price-setting hours since 2023. Both countries buy gas at hub prices that track each other, so the UK–German wholesale difference is a merit-order difference, not a fuel-price one — and Britain’s is partly self-inflicted: the £18 Carbon Price Support on top of the EU price pushed coal out of the British merit order years before Germany’s fleet lost the role. Carbon is no footnote in that price — 26 to 28% of British gas SRMC in 2023–25, £24 of £85/MWh in 2025.

Levelised cost — LCOE, the all-in cost of building and running a plant per unit of output — belongs here as the anchor economics, not the price mechanism. China does not run this auction at all: its tariff is anchored on coal generating at roughly $67/MWh inside a regulated band with no carbon cost, against an all-in cost of British gas-fired generation near $186. That anchor difference, not market-design detail, is the China gap of section 3. And cheap renewables do not pull the hourly price down while gas is marginal: their low LCOE governs what gets built and what CfDs cost, a saving that lands on the levy side of the bill — not on what you pay per hour.

7The verdict — and the three remedies it has to judge

The diagnosis comes back in three parts, depending on which comparison you care about.

Against China, the gap is the price of energy itself — gas-set wholesale against a coal anchor, plus a levy stack China does not carry. No relief scheme touches it.

Against Germany, for firms outside the relief schemes, the culprit is the levy stack — above all the cost of paying for the renewables buildout.

For fully exempt heavy industry — the firms at the centre of the deindustrialisation debate — the popular story is out of date. Levies are down to nothing, network charges have converged, and all-in Britain has crossed over — about $8/MWh cheaper than Germany at large size and nearly $50 at medium, where German relief largely passes two-shift sites by. What remains above the modelled line is procurement timing and the composition of the survey, not a policy charge.

So the claims come back mixed. Renewables levies did drive the pre-relief British price up, and still do for the 85% of industrial volume that holds no certificate. But for the exempt minority the levy lever is pulled to its stop, and the binding constraint is the wholesale price — gas plus carbon, almost to the pound.

That reframes the remedy debate. The three proposals in circulation — build nuclear, drill the North Sea, fix the grid — are not equally addressed to that constraint, and each can be tested against the mechanism it would have to move. Only one of them acts on the marginal plant.

8Remedy one: what nuclear actually costs

If the long-run answer to a gas-set power price is something cheaper at the margin, nuclear is the only firm low-carbon candidate — so what it costs decides whether the remedy works. Three tiers sit on the axis below, and only the distance between the last two matters: what the West has actually delivered runs $80–150/MWh above what construction costs where building is routine, for the same machines. The cheapest tier — life extension, or restarting a closed fleet — is not open to Britain, whose stations closed at the end of their lives.

That spread is not technology. France’s EPR models at $91/MWh at benchmark costs and China built two EPRs at Taishan in nine years; the same design took 17.7 years at Olkiluoto and slipped twelve at Flamanville. The spread is delivery — serial construction by an experienced supply chain against first-of-a-kind projects a generation after the last one — compounded by financing, since at Britain’s ~9% hurdle rates every year of delay capitalises into the strike price.

The schedule record also corrects a tempting shortcut — that China simply knows how to build and the West does not. China’s own 2009-vintage units sit in the slow half: Taishan and Sanmen were the first EPRs and AP1000s finished anywhere, and they slipped four to five years, spending nearly as long in construction as Vogtle. What changed by the 2015–2020 starts was the maturity of the product, not the nationality of the builder — the Hualong One is a domestic design built in series, and its newest units deliver in five to six and a half years. Even the engineering state bought its learning curve with a decade of late first-of-a-kinds. The experience is partly transferable — the Emirates hired Korea’s production line mid-run and built Barakah in under nine years with no nuclear history of its own — but no Western vendor has a warm line to hire, which makes the Sizewell C question precise: the second unit of a series, or another first one.

Two implications follow for the price. A buildout lowers wholesale only when there are enough reactors that gas stops setting the price — the role Germany’s coal fleet played in the 2010s and France’s reactors played for decades — so a partial programme delivers low-carbon energy without touching the marginal price at all. And since new nuclear is bought on contracts for difference, a buildout at too high a strike moves money from the wholesale line to the levy line rather than removing it.

So the placement against this study’s two reference lines is sharp. At the costs Britain has revealed, nuclear costs about what British gas-fired power costs: it insures against gas but cannot undercut it. At benchmark-tier costs it sits at or below the Chinese coal anchor, the line the whole China gap hangs from. The difference between those worlds is a construction industry, not physics — and financing is the one lever policy holds directly, which is what Sizewell C’s regulated-asset-base model is for.

9Remedy two: what’s left under the North Sea

The second remedy reaches for the gas itself, and the data gives it less room than the rhetoric. The UK Continental Shelf (UKCS) still supplies half of British gas consumption, but production, the inventory behind it and the capital spending that would replace it are all falling together — proven gas reserves now cover about three years of production and eighteen months of consumption.

The often-quoted doubling of unit operating cost — £11 a barrel in 2020 to £19.5 in 2024 (North Sea Transition Authority (NSTA) benchmarking, mixed oil-and-gas basis) — is that decline seen from the other side: total operating spend is flat in cash terms and a quarter lower in real terms, so the rise is the denominator shrinking, not the platforms getting dearer to run. And because the therm sells at the market price regardless, pulling unit cost back down widens the producer’s rent rather than narrowing anyone’s bill.

That is the mechanism the whole remedy runs into. Gas flows to the best price through interconnectors and liquefied-natural-gas (LNG) terminals, so an extra British therm displaces an imported one without discounting the marginal one — which makes drilling a question about tax receipts, the current account and security of supply, not about the power price. Even an implausible doubling of UKCS output would move the traded market by low single digits. America’s shale decade, the one modern case of drilling into cheap gas, needed production at world scale and export bottlenecks that trapped the gas at home; Britain has neither. The 2022 exception maps the limit: Britain’s hub briefly traded below the continent’s because LNG cargoes kept landing while the export pipes ran full — not because of drilling — and by 2023 the two were back within a dollar.

The proven stock is not the ceiling — the discovered-but-unsanctioned resource is more than three times larger — and what stalled is drilling, not geology: exploration’s yield has held at a ~50% success rate and £2–4 per barrel for two decades, while exploration and appraisal wells fell from 49 in 2010 to 7 in 2024. More spending would therefore find and sanction more gas — the scenario fans span 2.6 billion barrels of oil equivalent produced to 2050 with no further sanctioning against 4.3 billion on an accelerated path — and every barrel of that range runs into the same wall.

Nor is extraction cost the problem, as is often assumed. Like-for-like unit costs by country are not constructable, but delivered cost by route is, and it points the other way: British domestic gas is the cheapest route at under $5/MMBtu, with Qatari LNG and Norwegian pipe within a dollar of it, while US LNG — the cargo that balances Europe’s market — runs near $9, of which about $5 is liquefaction, shipping and regasification a domestic therm never incurs. The market price in 2025 was around $12. Every route sits far below it, because the price is set by the dearest supply plus scarcity and every cheaper route pockets the difference as rent. Extraction cost is not Britain’s gas problem; who collects the rent is.

Which reframes the most radical proposal — nationalise production and sell domestic gas at cost — as a rent transfer with a computable ceiling, not a cost reduction. Of the roughly £10/MWh wedge between 2025’s market price and full-cycle cost, the 78% marginal tax regime already assigns about £8 to the Exchequer — so selling the entire 344 TWh of domestic output at cost would transfer at most ~£3.5 billion a year to consumers, about £5/MWh across everything Britain burns, and largely from taxpayers rather than from new value. The wedge is a snapshot: with the market it has swung the Exchequer’s UKCS take between a £0.4 billion refund and £9 billion a year within the decade. A state owner would inherit the basin’s decommissioning liability besides, and the duration is capped by the inventory — eighteen months of consumption in proven gas, sustained only by the investment whose returns the scheme would confiscate. Norway is the control case: the cheapest producer in Europe sells at market and banks the rent in a sovereign fund.

10Remedy three: the grid

The third remedy is the one the popular diagnosis rests on: that network costs are rising because grids rebuilt around intermittent generation are less utilised, so filling the wires back up would cheapen them. Strip everything except the network and balancing charges out of the section-3 stacks and the claim is testable directly.

Network and balancing charges only — transmission and distribution use-of-system, balancing and the hydro-benefit levy for the UK; Netzentgelte plus the §19, offshore and interruptible-load surcharges for Germany. Unrelieved solid, relieved dashed. Germany’s 2024 doubling is the Karlsruhe budget ruling: the federal transmission subsidy died with the 2024 tariff round (final joint price sheet, 13.12.2023), stayed dead in 2025, and returns at €6.5bn for 2026, halving the charge. Tariff sources: NESO and Northern Powergrid charging statements; joint ÜNB and Westnetz price sheets.

The British large user’s grid bill nearly quadrupled in real terms, to $30/MWh, and the unrelieved medium site carries the heaviest burden of the four cases at $37. Relief works — from April 2026 a qualifying site pays a tenth of the residual — and, least expected, unrelieved British grid charges are lower than German ones at both archetypes. The two countries also disagree about who deserves relief: Germany conditions its discount on load shape, Britain on trade exposure. Germany pays firms for being good grid citizens; Britain pays them for being exposed.

The utilisation theory itself does not survive. The one measure published — system load factor, delivered energy against annual peak — has barely moved in fifteen years (0.70 to 0.67 on DUKES data). How intensively the wires themselves are used, no British source publishes: Ofgem collects circuit loadings from the network owners and does not release them, so claims about idle wires cannot be tested either way. What can be tested is the term that actually enters the charge, since a charge is allowed revenue divided by the base it is spread over. Between charging years 2020/21 and 2025/26 the transmission revenue to collect rose 79% — the demand half of it by 60% — while every candidate base fell: national consumption by 5.5%, and the published demand charging bases by 3% to 14% (NESO’s Final TNUoS Tariffs reports, one per charging year). In logs the numerator carries between two-thirds and nine-tenths of the rise depending on which base you spread it over, and the shrinking base the remainder. The base is real — Ofgem warns that “fixed costs are recovered over a shrinking base of megawatt hours” (ED3 methodology decision, May 2026) — but it is the minor term, and its German mirror cuts against the idle-grid framing entirely: the BDEW-commissioned Frontier/Consentec study finds network cost per kilowatt of peak load falling to 2045, with per-kWh charges rising because exemptions and self-consumption shrink the billed base faster than the used one.

The same British charges split into wires (use-of-system) and balancing (BSUoS), unrelieved, on one axis. The medium site’s wires bill is lower in real terms than in 2010 — the 2022 step is the Targeted Charging Review moving the distribution residual into fixed charges — so its entire net rise is balancing, whose share went from 9% to 47%. Balancing outturn and curtailment: NESO Annual Balancing Costs Report 2025 data workbook.

What the data does support is the balancing line. Two-thirds of everything added to the large user’s grid bill since 2010 is BSUoS: thermal constraint costs went from £446m in 2018/19 to £1,709m in 2024/25, and the share of British wind output paid to switch off rose from 5% to 13% in two years. That payment is a market-design choice, not physics: Britain has one national price and sells generators firm access, so when the Scotland–England boundary is full the National Energy System Operator (NESO) must buy back positions the market has already sold, at bids that recover the subsidy a curtailed wind farm forgoes. Under zonal pricing no cheque would exist — the local price would simply fall — and the government weighed exactly that transfer of risk in July 2025’s Review of Electricity Market Arrangements and kept national pricing, accepting the constraint bill as the price of investor certainty.

The forward story is construction and financing, not utilisation either. The roughly £20bn accelerated transmission programme exists precisely to kill the constraint bill, and Ofgem expects constraints to fall from around £8bn to £3bn a year by 2030. But of the £108 the 2026–31 price control adds to an average household’s annual network charges, £31 is the extra investment and £48 is cost of capital and depreciation policy (Ofgem impact assessment, Table 2) — the same grid, financed at the top of the rate cycle, costs more than it would have five years ago.

As a competitiveness lever the grid mostly fails the mechanism test for the firms the debate worries about: their charges are compensated at 90% from April 2026 — worth £23–31/MWh to the same few hundred certificated firms — leaving £2–3. Against Germany the premise inverts outright. Three targets survive: the unrelieved middle, which carries the heaviest grid burden of anyone and whose new industrial-competitiveness scheme covers levies but explicitly not network charges; the constraint bill, the one line that is a real resource cost rather than a transfer between bill-payers; and the financing terms, which added more to the coming increase than the construction did and have had far less scrutiny.

11Conclusions

Britain did not price itself out of contention. China priced itself in. A fully relieved British site pays $118/MWh on the statutory stack in 2025 — less than it paid in 2012 ($130). Over the same fifteen years the Chinese industrial tariff fell from $145 to $87, down 40%. The gap between them swung by $47/MWh, and China’s decline accounts for $58 of that while Britain improved by $11. For the firms this debate is actually about, the competitiveness problem is not something British energy policy did to them. It is something Chinese electricity supply did — and it keeps widening even if Western prices return fully to their pre-crisis baseline.

Before the crisis, relief did its job. The headline price was a policy choice — levies loaded onto electricity to fund the renewables rollout — but both countries then exempted the industry that choice would otherwise have killed. On exempt rates Britain sat broadly level with the Chinese tariff through the 2010s, and Germany ran $35–59/MWh below it from 2014 to 2018. Whatever drove British industrial decline before 2020, the electricity bill of a covered firm is not a persuasive candidate — though relief reaches only about 15% of industrial volume, so that is true of a covered minority, not of the sector.

Since 2020 the binding constraint has been wholesale — and wholesale is gas plus carbon. Covid and Ukraine were shocks to gas that passed through even fully exempted bills. Stabilisation plus deeper exemptions should return industrial prices roughly to their pre-crisis baseline; they will not return them to parity with China. And the relief lever is spent: depth is at its statutory maximum, so the marginal pound buys coverage, not depth. Yet 85% of industrial volume is still bought at unrelieved rates, and no register exists to say how much of that is ineligibility and how much is eligible firms never applying — establishing which is the cheapest piece of analysis anywhere in this subject. For those outside, the levy principal is already committed, so the only lever left on it is the uprating: April 2026’s switch of the Renewables Obligation buy-out price from RPI to CPI indexation, worth about £270 million a year at its 2030 peak (DESNZ government response).

The largest short-run movement needs no policy at all. The 2022-vintage contracts behind the £130 plateau of section 5 roll off on their own, and the first 2026 print has implied prices falling 15–17% year on year. That is worth more to British industrial bills over the next two years than any measure discussed here — and it sets a test: if the full-year prints do not follow the market down, the problem is contracting structure rather than tax or levy, and it wants a different instrument.

Carbon is the largest lever policy holds directly, and the one that cannot be aimed. It is £24/MWh — 28% of British gas marginal cost, about $32, and the only lever here that reaches relieved and unrelieved firms alike, because it acts on the wholesale price rather than on a bill line that relief can waive. But that symmetry cuts both ways, and hardest against this study’s own subject: the European Union prices the same carbon at the border, and emissions-trading linkage — which would exempt British exporters — is premised on comparable carbon pricing. Cutting the domestic price on steel, cement or glass would therefore relocate much of the payment to the border while forfeiting the revenue. The legislated partial version already exists: the £18 Carbon Price Support ends in April 2028.

Renewables have to pass the same test as nuclear, and currently fail it for the same reason. They are the closest match to the Chinese coal anchor on levelised cost — British onshore wind at $62/MWh, solar $67, offshore $72, against coal at $67 — but low levelised cost does not move the hourly price while gas is marginal. It governs what gets built and what contracts for difference cost, so the saving lands on the levy line. In the near term the rollout is a fuel saver — import substitution and a smaller levy bill, not a cheaper megawatt-hour — and it becomes a price cut only when there is enough of it that gas stops setting the margin, the identical threshold section 8 set for reactors. Meanwhile roughly half the balancing bill is constraint cost, which is a failure to build wires on time rather than an inherent cost of intermittency. Continue the rollout, then, but grid build is the binding constraint on its value.

The other two remedies do less than advertised. Nuclear changes nothing this decade whatever is decided now — the cheapest tier, life extension and restart, is largely Germany’s option rather than Britain’s, and new build turns on whether Sizewell C becomes the second unit of a series. The North Sea is a fiscal measure, not an energy one: drilling does not move the price of the marginal therm, the contingent resource adds about five years of gas demand on top of proven reserves, and the case for it rests on Exchequer revenue whose yield has swung between a £0.4 billion refund and £9 billion a year within the decade. It should be argued as revenue policy and not as a pillar of energy security.

The verdict on policy is better than the debate allows, with one qualification. After two genuine errors — loading the renewables buildout onto electricity bills, and the nuclear phase-out — the response to the crisis has been competent: first deepening industrial relief, now the roughly £20bn accelerated transmission programme that Ofgem expects to cut constraint costs from around £8bn to £3bn a year by 2030. The qualification is that relief is a transfer, not a saving: every exempted pound is recovered from consumers who do not qualify, so it made covered industry competitive without making British electricity cheaper. And both programmes now depend on something neither has demonstrated — control of infrastructure cost. Of the £108 the 2026–31 price control adds to an average household’s annual network charges, £31 is the extra investment and £48 is cost of capital and depreciation policy: the least-scrutinised line in the argument.

So the list is short, and it is not the list the debate starts from. Continue the renewables rollout but make grid build the priority, because the rollout’s price effect is gated on it. Widen relief coverage rather than deepen it. Allow North Sea drilling as a fiscal measure, explicitly not as energy or security policy. Commit to a long-term nuclear strategy judged on serial delivery and financing terms rather than headline capital cost. And accept what the arithmetic says about the destination: the wholesale price stays the gas price until something cheaper sets the margin — a construction problem, slow and impossible to legislate into effect within a parliament, but the only route that changes the answer for every firm at once instead of redistributing between them.

What would overturn this. A wholesale market where gas stops setting the price in most hours, which would make the levy stack binding again. Or evidence that the unrelieved 85% is unrelieved by choice rather than by rule, which would make Britain’s problem far smaller than it looks. Both are measurable. Neither is measured today.
